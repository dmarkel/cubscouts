Bobcat · Leader GuideAt a glance

The big idea

Bobcat is the first adventure every Webelos earns, and it sets the tone for the whole year. It's about who we are as a den: we get to know each other, we learn the promises Scouts make (the Scout Oath and Scout Law), we decide together how we'll treat each other (our Code of Conduct), and we start sharing leadership (the denner). Most 4th graders have heard the Oath and Law before. This year, the goal is for them to understand what the words mean and to start living them.

Goals for your scouts

  • Belong: Every scout knows everyone's name and something about them, and feels like part of the den.
  • Promise: Recite the Scout Oath and Law, and explain the three duties in the Oath in their own words.
  • Understand: Explain what each point of the Scout Law looks like in real life.
  • Lead: Know what a denner does and get excited to take a turn.
  • Own it: Help write the den's Code of Conduct and agree to follow it.
  • Show it: Use the Cub Scout sign, salute, and handshake correctly and know when to use each.

Bobcat · Leader GuideRequirement 1
1
Requirement 1Get to know members of your den.

Goal: Every scout learns everyone's name and at least one fun fact about each den member. A den where scouts feel known is a den where they behave better and want to come back.

"A den is like a team. Good teams know each other, including what everybody is good at, what they like, and what makes them laugh. This year we're going to camp together, build things together, and maybe get a little muddy together. So first, let's find out who's on our team!"

Human Bingo (10 min, gathering). Print the bingo sheet at the back of this guide. Scouts find someone who matches each square and have them sign it. Each person can sign only one square per sheet. First row across, down, or diagonal wins, but keep playing until most squares are full. Afterward, ask: "Who found someone surprising?"

Name Toss (5 min). Stand in a circle. Say your name and your favorite food, then toss the ball to someone else. Round 2: say the catcher's name and their favorite food before you toss. Round 3: go faster!

Two Truths and a Tall Tale (5 min). Each scout shares three "facts" about themselves, two true and one made up. The den guesses which is the tall tale. Leaders go first to model it.

  • Can each scout name every member of the den? Go around the circle to check.
  • Ask: "What's something you have in common with someone you didn't know before?"

New scouts in the den?

Pair each new scout with a returning scout as their "den buddy" for the first month. The buddy helps them learn the Oath, Law, and sign, and makes sure they're never standing alone.

Bobcat · Leader GuideRequirement 2
2
Requirement 2Recite the Scout Oath and the Scout Law with your den and den leader. Describe 3 points of the Scout Oath.

Goal: Scouts can say the Oath and Law with the den and explain, in their own words, the three duties the Oath promises: duty to God and country, duty to others, and duty to self.

The Scout Oath

On my honor I will do my best
to do my duty to God and my country and to obey the Scout Law;
to help other people at all times;
to keep myself physically strong, mentally awake, and morally straight.

"An oath is a big promise. When you say 'On my honor,' you're saying, 'You can count on me.' And 'I will do my best' is our Cub Scout motto: nobody's perfect, but we always try. The Oath has three big promises in it, which we call duties:"

  • Duty to God and country: Practice your family's faith and respect other people's beliefs. Be a good citizen: respect the flag, follow the laws, and help your community.
  • Duty to others: Help people all the time, not just when someone's watching or when you feel like it.
  • Duty to self: Take care of yourself. Physically strong means eat healthy, exercise, and sleep. Mentally awake means keep learning and stay curious. Morally straight means do the right thing and be honest, even when it's hard.

Oath Scramble (8 min). Write each line of the Oath on a paper strip (6 strips per set). Teams race to put their strips in order, then read it aloud together. Round 2: flip a strip face down and recite the missing line from memory.

Three Duties Sort (8 min). Label three buckets God & Country, Others, Self. Read each action aloud; scouts toss a beanbag into the bucket where it fits (some fit more than one, so talk about it!):

Stand quietly for the flag (Country)
Pick up trash at a park (Country/Others)
Carry groceries for a neighbor (Others)
Sit with a new kid at lunch (Others)
Go to bed on time (Self: physically strong)
Read a book for fun (Self: mentally awake)
Tell the truth when you broke something (Self: morally straight)
Attend worship with your family (God)
Learn the Pledge of Allegiance (Country)
Play outside instead of screens (Self: physically strong)

  • Recite the Oath and Law together as a den. Sign off scouts who participate.
  • Ask each scout to describe the three duties in their own words, or name one thing they could do this week for each.

On "duty to God": Scouting is non-sectarian. Let families lead on faith, and never ask scouts to share their personal beliefs in front of the group.

Requirement 3Learn about the Scout Law.

Goal: Scouts understand what each of the 12 points means and can give an example of it from their own lives.

"The Scout Oath is our promise. The Scout Law is our how-to guide: 12 words that describe the kind of person a Scout is. Notice it doesn't say 'A Scout tries to be...' It says 'A Scout is.' These are things you can be every day, at school, at home, and on the playground."

Scout Law Relay (gathering). Two teams, two sets of Scout Law cards (printable at the back of this guide). Race to lay them out in order.

Which Point Is It? (10 min). Hand each scout a few Scout Law cards. Read a situation; scouts hold up the point it shows. "You found a wallet and turned it in" (Trustworthy). "You tried a hard hike even though you were nervous" (Brave). "You shared your snack with someone who forgot theirs" (Kind/Helpful). "You used both sides of the paper" (Thrifty). "You said 'good game' to the other team after losing" (Courteous/Cheerful).

Scout Law Charades (8 min). A scout draws a card and acts out that point with no words. The den guesses.

Ask each scout: "Which point of the Scout Law is easiest for you? Which one do you want to work on?"

Bobcat · Leader GuideRequirement 4
4
Requirement 4Learn about the denner position and responsibilities.

Goal: Scouts know what a denner does, why it matters, and that everyone will get a turn. This is the first step toward the youth-led patrols they'll join in Scouts BSA.

"In Webelos, scouts start doing some of the leading. The denner is a den member who helps lead the den for a while, and then the job passes to someone else, so everybody gets a turn. When you're denner, the den is counting on you to set a good example."

What a denner does

  • Helps the den leader set up and clean up
  • Leads the flag ceremony, Oath, or Law at the opening
  • Helps lead games and activities
  • Helps the den stay on track using the Code of Conduct
  • Represents the den at pack meetings
  • Sets a good example by living the Scout Law

How to set it up

  • Rotate on a schedule you pick (for example, monthly), so every scout serves during the year.
  • Choose the first denner by den vote or by drawing names.
  • Some dens also have an assistant denner who becomes the next denner.
  • If your pack uses one, the denner wears the denner shoulder cord during their term.
  • Record the rotation on the tracking sheet.

Denner Job Jar (5 min). Put slips in a jar: "Lead the Scout Oath," "Hand out supplies," "Lead the cleanup," "Explain a game," "Welcome a visitor." Scouts draw one and do it right then. Talk about it: "Which job was easy? Which was hard? What makes a good leader?"

Choose the first denner (5 min). Hold the vote or drawing, and present the cord if you have one. Their first job is to lead the closing today.

  • Name two things a denner does.
  • Why does the denner job rotate? (So everyone gets to practice leading.)
  • Which point of the Scout Law is most important for a denner? (Any good answer, such as Helpful, Trustworthy, or Courteous.)
Bobcat · Leader GuideRequirement 5
5
Requirement 5With your den, create a den Code of Conduct.

Goal: The den writes its own rules for how they'll treat each other, so scouts own them. Scouts follow rules they helped write far better than rules handed to them.

"Every good team has rules everybody agrees on. Tonight, you get to write ours. Think about what makes a den meeting fun and fair for everyone, and what makes it not fun. Our rules should match the Scout Law."

How to run it (15 min)

  1. Brainstorm. Ask: "What makes a great den meeting? What ruins one?" Write every idea on a whiteboard.
  2. Make it positive. Turn "Don't interrupt" into "We listen when someone is talking." Turn "No hitting" into "We keep our hands to ourselves."
  3. Keep it short. Combine ideas into 5–8 rules. Scouts should be able to remember them.
  4. Connect it to the Scout Law. For each rule, ask "Which point of the Scout Law is this?"
  5. Agree on what happens if someone forgets a rule. For example: 1st, a friendly reminder (the Cub Scout sign); 2nd, a short time-out; 3rd, a talk with parents.
  6. Sign it. The denner writes the final rules on poster board, and every scout and leader signs it.

Example den Code of Conduct

  1. We listen when someone is talking. When the sign goes up, we get quiet. (Courteous)
  2. We keep our hands and feet to ourselves. (Kind)
  3. We use kind words, with no put-downs. (Friendly, Clean)
  4. We include everyone. (Friendly)
  5. We take care of our stuff and help clean up. (Thrifty, Helpful)
  6. We follow our leaders' directions and stay safe. (Obedient)
  7. We always do our best. (Cub Scout motto)

Thumbs Vote. Read each proposed rule; scouts vote thumbs up, sideways, or down. For any sideways or down votes, ask "How could we change it so you'd give a thumbs up?" This teaches that leaders listen, too.

Bring the poster to every meeting. If behavior slips, point to the poster instead of lecturing: "Which of our rules do we need right now?"

Requirement 6Demonstrate the Cub Scout sign, Cub Scout salute, and Cub Scout handshake. Show how each is used.

Goal: Scouts can correctly do all three and know when each one is used.

 How to do itWhen we use it
Cub Scout signRaise your right arm straight up. Hold up your first two fingers (index and middle) in a V, with your thumb holding down your other two fingers.When saying the Scout Oath and Law, and as the quiet signal: when a leader raises the sign, everyone raises theirs and gets quiet.
Cub Scout saluteMake the same hand shape, but put your first two fingers together. Touch your fingertips to your right eyebrow (or the brim of your cap).To salute the flag when in uniform, and to show respect to leaders. (Out of uniform, place your right hand over your heart for the flag.)
Cub Scout handshakeShake right hands as usual, but put your first two fingers straight along the inside of the other person's wrist.Greeting other Cub Scouts and leaders, and when receiving an award.

"The two fingers in the Cub Scout sign stand for the ears of a wolf, alert and listening. That's why we use it to get quiet: when the sign goes up, our ears are open and our mouths are closed."

Leader Says (8 min). Like Simon Says: "Leader says, give the Cub Scout salute!" Mix in "Leader says, show the sign," "Leader says, shake hands with the person to your left," and trick commands without "Leader says." Let the denner or scouts take turns being the leader.

Quiet Sign Challenge. Throughout the meeting, raise the sign at random moments. Time how fast the whole den gets quiet with their signs up. Can they beat their record next meeting?

Handshake Line. Scouts line up and shake hands with every leader using the Cub Scout handshake, looking them in the eye and saying "Hello, I'm ____."

  • Watch each scout do all three correctly.
  • Ask: "When do we use the sign? The salute? The handshake?"
  • What's the difference between the sign and the salute? (Fingers apart vs. together, and arm up vs. at the eyebrow.)

Requirement 7At home, with your parent or legal guardian, do the activities in the booklet How to Protect Your Children from Child Abuse: A Parent's Guide.

Goal: Families have an age-appropriate conversation about personal safety at home, where the parent knows their child best.

The leader's role

  • Don't teach this in the den meeting. It is designed to happen at home, between the scout and their parent or guardian.
  • Send home the Parent Letter. The booklet is in the front of the Webelos Handbook and is also available on Scouting America's website.
  • Mark the requirement complete when the parent or guardian confirms it's done (the tear-off slip on the letter works well).
  • Follow up kindly with families who haven't finished; offer another copy of the booklet if needed.

If a scout ever tells you something that makes you think they may have been abused, stay calm, listen, and don't investigate. Follow your Youth Protection training: report it right away to local law enforcement or child protective services, and notify your Scout Executive. Every adult leader must have current Youth Protection training.

Cubmaster's Minute: "The Bobcat"

"Did you know a bobcat is small, only about twice the size of a house cat, but it's one of the most respected animals in the forest? It's not the biggest or the loudest. It's respected because it's smart, it's careful, and it's always alert. That's what the Bobcat adventure is about. You don't have to be the biggest or the loudest kid in your class to be a leader. You have to keep your promises, like the ones in the Scout Oath. You have to live the Scout Law even when nobody's watching. And you have to always do your best. Do that, and you'll be the kind of Scout everyone respects."
