Aquanaut · Leader GuideAt a glance

The big idea

Aquanaut teaches Webelos that water is fun when we're smart about it. By the end, every scout should be able to tell you the safety rules we follow before anyone gets wet, how to help a struggling swimmer without becoming a second victim, how to spot and stop getting dangerously cold, and should have tried the same swim test used across Scouting America.

Goals for your scouts

  • Know the rules: Explain, in their own words, why we use Safe Swim Defense, ability groups, and the buddy system.
  • Help safely: Know the order of rescue (Reach, Throw, Row, Go) and that a Cub Scout's job is to Reach or Throw — but Don't Go.
  • Stay warm: Recognize the warning signs of hypothermia (shivering and "the Umbles") and know what to do.
  • Try their best: Attempt treading water and the swimmer test — the requirement is to attempt, so effort earns it.
  • Practice it for real: Use the buddy system and ability groups during at least 30 minutes of free swim.

Other ways a scout can earn Aquanaut

Per the Webelos handbook, a scout may also earn this adventure by passing the BSA swimmer test or by taking swimming lessons from a qualified instructor (e.g., a Red Cross or YMCA program). If a scout already qualifies, still invite them to every activity — strong swimmers make great "safety assistants" for the dry-land stations.

Always confirm requirement wording against the current Webelos Handbook or Scouting America's online adventure page; requirements are summarized here for planning.

Safety & planning for adults

Every Scouting swim activity follows Safe Swim Defense. The adult in charge of the swim must have current Safe Swim Defense training (free online at my.scouting.org). Plan swim day at a pool or waterfront with trained lifeguards on duty, and check with the facility about how they handle Scouting swim tests.

Planning timeline

Swim classifications are typically renewed each year. The facility sets exact area boundaries — follow their lifeguards.

Keep it positive

Some 4th graders are nervous about water or embarrassed that they can't swim yet. Say it plainly and often: "Every requirement that says 'attempt' is about trying. Doing your best earns it." Never announce test results to the group, never rank scouts, and never pressure a scout into deeper water. A nonswimmer who puts their face in the water for the first time had a huge win — celebrate it like one.

Youth protection reminders for swim day

  • Two registered adults present at all times; no one-on-one contact.
  • Appropriate swimwear for everyone. Changing areas: adults and youth change separately; adults do not change or shower with youth.
  • Photos: only with families' permission and never in changing areas.

Aquanaut · Leader GuideRequirement 1
1
Requirement 1State the safety precautions you need to take before doing any swimming activity.

Goal: Scouts can name the safety steps Scouting uses for every swim (Safe Swim Defense), and can explain why the buddy system and ability groups matter.

"Swimming is one of the most fun things we do in Scouting. But water is also one of the few places where something can go wrong fast and quietly — a person who is drowning usually can't yell or wave. So Scouting has a plan we use every single time we swim. It's called Safe Swim Defense, and it has 8 parts. Here's a silly sentence to help you remember them:"

Quick Pups Swim Rapidly, Lazy Alligators Bask Daily.

 Safe Swim Defense pointWhat it means (kid version)
QQualified SupervisionA trained adult is in charge of the swim.
PPersonal Health ReviewAdults know about your health. Tell them if you feel sick or have a health issue.
SSafe AreaThe swim area has been checked: we know how deep it is, and there's nothing dangerous in it.
RResponse Personnel (Lifeguards)Trained lifeguards are there and ready to help.
LLookoutSomeone watches the whole area from a spot where they can see everybody.
AAbility GroupsYou swim only where your swim test says you can: nonswimmer, beginner, or swimmer area.
BBuddy SystemNever swim alone. Stay with your buddy the whole time and answer every buddy check.
DDisciplineFollow the rules and the lifeguard's directions. No running, pushing, or dunking.

Why buddies? "Your buddy is your personal lifeguard and you are theirs. You stay close enough to reach each other. When the adult calls 'Buddy check!' you grab your buddy's hand, raise it high, and freeze and stay quiet so the adults can count. If someone's buddy is missing, the adults find out in seconds, not minutes."

Safe Swim Scramble (8 min). Write each of the 8 points on an index card, with the kid-version meaning on a second card (16 cards). Shuffle and hand them out. Scouts find their match, then line up in "Quick Pups" order and each pair reads their card aloud.

Buddy Check Drill (5 min). Pair scouts up. Let them move around the room ("swimming"). Blow the whistle or call "Buddy check!" — pairs grab hands, raise them, and freeze. Time them; try to beat the den's record. Secretly send one scout to hide behind a door on one round and ask: "What should happen right now?" (Everyone stops; adults search immediately.)

  • What do you do when you hear "Buddy check"? (Grab buddy's hand, raise it, stay still and quiet.)
  • Why can't a beginner swim in the deep end just because a lifeguard is watching? (Ability groups keep you in water you've proven you can handle.)
  • Name 3 things that have to happen before anyone gets in the water.
Aquanaut · Leader GuideRequirement 2
2
Requirement 2Explain the meaning of "order of rescue" and demonstrate the reach and throw rescue techniques from land.

Goal: Scouts can list the order of rescue, understand that it goes from safest to riskiest, and can show a reach and a throw rescue from land. Most importantly: Cub Scouts never swim out to rescue someone.

"Here's a scary fact: lots of people who drown were trying to rescue someone else. A scared person in the water will grab anything — including you — and pull you under. So rescuers follow an order, starting with the safest way to help:"

  1. REACH — Lie down on the deck or dock and reach out with a pole, noodle, towel, or even your arm or leg. Stay low so you don't get pulled in.
  2. THROW — If they're too far to reach, throw something that floats: a ring buoy, a throw rope, a life jacket, even a cooler or jug. Hang on to the end of the rope!
  3. ROW — A trained adult can use a boat to get to them.
  4. GO — Only a trained lifeguard swims out, and they bring something that floats.

"Your rule as a Webelos is: Yell for help, then Reach or Throw — but Don't Go!"

How to do it right

Reach

  • Shout for an adult/lifeguard first.
  • Get low: lie flat or kneel, or hold onto something solid (ladder, railing, a friend holding your legs).
  • Extend the object; say "Grab on!"
  • Pull them slowly to the edge.
  • If you feel yourself being pulled in, let go.

Throw

  • Shout for help first.
  • Hold the end of the rope or step on it.
  • Yell "Rope!" so they're ready.
  • Throw past the person so the rope lands across them — not at their head.
  • Crouch low and pull in slowly, hand over hand.

Reach & Throw Stations (15 min). Lay a hula hoop or tarp on the floor about 8 ft away — that's "the water." A volunteer (adult or scout) sits in it as the "swimmer" and acts panicked.

  • Station A – Reach: Scout yells "Help! Lifeguard!", lies down on the "deck," reaches out with a pool noodle or towel, and pulls the swimmer in. Swap roles.
  • Station B – Throw: Scout holds the rope end, yells "Rope!", and throws the jug so the rope lands across the swimmer. Pull in. Move the hoop farther away each round for a challenge.
  • Bonus – Look around: Scatter objects (backpack, jacket, water bottle, ball, pillow) and ask "Which of these could you use to reach? To throw?"

Sign off requirement 2 when each scout explains the order and completes one reach and one throw.

  • Why is "Reach" first and "Go" last? (Reach is safest for the rescuer; going in the water is the most dangerous.)
  • Why do you lie down to do a reach? (So you don't get pulled in.)
  • What's the very first thing to do? (Shout for an adult or lifeguard.)
Aquanaut · Leader GuideRequirement 3
3
Requirement 3Learn how to prevent and treat hypothermia.

Goal: Scouts know what hypothermia is, can name the early warning signs, and know how to prevent it and what to do if a buddy is getting too cold.

"Hypothermia (hi-po-THER-mee-uh) means your body is losing heat faster than it can make it, and your insides start getting too cold. Water pulls heat out of you about 25 times faster than air does, so you can get hypothermia even on a warm day if you stay in the water too long or sit around wet in the wind."

"The first sign is shivering — that's your body's alarm bell. If it keeps going, watch for the Umbles:"

  • Stumbles – clumsy, tripping
  • Mumbles – hard to talk clearly
  • Fumbles – fingers don't work right
  • Grumbles – cranky, confused

"Also look for blue or purple lips and fingers. People with hypothermia often don't notice it themselves — that's why buddies watch each other."

Prevent it

  • Get out of the water when you start shivering.
  • Towel off right away and put on dry clothes or a sweatshirt.
  • Stay out of the wind; wear a hat when it's cool.
  • Eat snacks and drink water — food is fuel for body heat.
  • Take breaks; don't stay in cold water a long time.

Treat it

  • Tell an adult right away.
  • Get out of the water and out of the wind.
  • Take off wet clothes; put on dry, warm layers or a blanket.
  • If they're awake and alert: a warm (not hot) drink and a snack.
  • Stumbling, confused, or very sleepy? That's serious — adults call 911.

Ice Water Fumble Challenge (6 min). Each scout first times how fast they can pick up 10 coins (or button 3 buttons on a shirt). Then they hold one hand in ice water for 30 seconds and try again with that hand. Talk about it: "Why was it harder? Imagine your whole body feeling like that." Keep it short, make it optional, and dry hands afterward.

Umbles Charades (4 min). Scouts draw a card — Stumbles, Mumbles, Fumbles, Grumbles, Shivering — and act it out. The den guesses, then shouts what to do: "Tell an adult! Get dry! Get warm!"

  • What is the first warning sign that you're getting too cold? (Shivering.)
  • Name two of the Umbles.
  • Your buddy's lips are blue and they're mumbling. What do you do? (Tell an adult, get them out of the water, dry and warm.)
Aquanaut · Leader GuideRequirement 4
4
Requirement 4Attempt to tread water.

Goal: Scouts try to stay upright with their head above water using slow, steady arm and leg movements. It's a key survival skill: if you fall in, treading water lets you stay up, breathe, and call for help.

"Treading water is like standing up in water that's too deep to stand in. The secret is slow and relaxed. Panicking and splashing wears you out fast."

  • Arms – "spread the peanut butter": Hands flat, just under the surface, sweep them out and in, like spreading peanut butter on a giant slice of bread.
  • Legs – pick one: Bicycle (pedal slowly), scissor (one leg forward, one back, snap together), or frog kick (knees out, kick around and together).
  • Body: Stay tall, chin at the water, breathe slow. Look at a friend on the deck and smile!

Tread & Talk. In each ability area (with the lifeguard's OK), scouts tread while answering easy questions called by a leader: "Favorite pizza topping? What's the Cub Scout motto?" Talking keeps them calm. Build up: 15 sec → 30 sec → 1 minute. Nonswimmers can hold the wall with one hand and practice the arms and legs — that's a real attempt.

Aquanaut · Leader GuideRequirement 5
5
Requirement 5Attempt the Scouting America (BSA) swimmer test.

Goal: Every scout tries the test in a supportive, low-pressure setting, and learns which ability group they're in and what to practice next.

The swimmer test

  1. Jump feet first into water over your head.
  2. Level off and swim 75 yards in a strong manner using one or more of: sidestroke, breaststroke, trudgen, or crawl.
  3. Then swim 25 yards using an easy, resting backstroke.
  4. The 100 yards must be one continuous swim with no stops and include at least one sharp turn.
  5. After the swim, rest by floating.

"This is the same test Scouts BSA and even adults take to swim in the deep area. It's long — 100 yards is four lengths of most pools. The requirement says attempt, so if you give it your best try, you've earned this part no matter how far you get. Whatever group you're in today, you'll know exactly what to practice to move up."

The test must be given by a qualified person (for example, the facility's lifeguard/aquatics staff or a council-approved test administrator) with lifeguards watching. The test administrator decides how each scout attempts it safely — a scout who isn't comfortable in deep water may start with the beginner test. Record each scout's result (Swimmer / Beginner / Nonswimmer) and the date.

Aquanaut · Leader GuideRequirement 6
6
Requirement 6Have 30 minutes, or more, of free swim time where you practice the buddy system and stay within your ability group. The qualified adult supervision should conduct at least three buddy checks per half hour of swimming.

Goal: Scouts use everything they learned, for real, while having fun. This is the "reward" — but the adults are watching for buddy habits.

"Now it's time to show you're real Aquanauts. You and your buddy stay together, stay in your area, and when you hear the whistle and 'Buddy check!' — hands up, freeze, quiet. We'll do at least three of these. Let's see if our den can be the fastest buddy check at the pool!"

Running a buddy check

  1. Blow one long whistle blast and call "Buddy check!"
  2. Buddies grab hands, raise them high, and stay still and quiet.
  3. The adult counts pairs and compares to the buddy board (the count must match the number checked in).
  4. If anyone is missing: everyone out of the water, lifeguards notified, search immediately.
  5. Call "All clear" and resume swimming.

Extra games & activities

Buddy Check Challenge (pool): Surprise buddy checks become a competition — fastest pair to get hands up and freeze earns a point for the den.

Treasure Dive (pool): Toss sinking rings or diving sticks into each ability area. Buddies take turns retrieving them. Keep items in water appropriate for each group.

Noodle Relay (pool, shallow): Buddies push a pool noodle across the area and back — no hands allowed, only blowing on it or nudging with noses/chins.

Float Contest (pool): Who can float on their back the longest while perfectly still? (Great practice for the end of the swimmer test.)

Rescue Relay (dry land, extra meeting time): Teams race to pick the right rescue object from a pile and complete a reach, then a throw, to "swimmers" in hula hoops.

Cubmaster's Minute: "Who's got your back?"

"At the pool, your buddy keeps you safe. But the buddy system doesn't stop at the water. At school, on the bus, on the playground — a good buddy notices when you're having a hard time and gets help. A good buddy doesn't let you do something dangerous. The Scout Law says a Scout is helpful and friendly. This week, be the kind of buddy you'd want in the water with you: someone who's always watching out for their friends."
